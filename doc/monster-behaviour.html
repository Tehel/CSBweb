How Dungeon Master's monsters think

A plain-language account of the decision-making behind the creatures in FTL's 1987 dungeon crawler — the senses they have, the moods they move between, and the handful of things that still make no sense.

Traced from Paul R. Stevens' CSBWin C++ source · Checked against ReDMCSB, the reconstruction of the Atari's own code · Behaviour observed running in the port

Everything here is behaviour I read in the source and then watched happen. Where I could not work out why something is the way it is, it goes in Unsolved at the end rather than being smoothed over, and what has since been explained is under Solved.

One machine, many parameters. I had expected each monster type to have its own logic and found the opposite: there is a single decision procedure, and the twenty-odd creature types differ almost entirely by the numbers fed into it. The exceptions — the giggler, Lord Chaos, and the handful of creatures that throw things — are called out below.

The unit of behaviour is the group, not the monster

A "monster" in the dungeon file is up to four creatures of one kind sharing a cell. They move as one, they are hurt as one, they have one shared mood. Only in a fight do they act individually, and even then only sometimes.

Each group occupies one cell and, within it, each member stands in one of four corners — or the whole group is "centred", which is what a solitary monster does. A worm is wide enough to occupy two corners; a dragon fills the cell entirely and can never be more than one.

The dungeon is not simulated behind you

A group only thinks while the party is on its level. Off-level, the group leader gets an occasional turn in which it takes one random step and then sleeps for a while — longer the further away the party is. Everything else is frozen. The dungeon does not live without you; it only twitches.

What a monster can perceive

Two senses, and they fail in different ways. That difference is doing more work than either sense on its own.

Sight

To see you, all of these must hold:

  1. You are not magically invisible — unless this creature sees through that.
  2. You are in front of it. Roughly a 45° cone, but one cell wider than that suggests: a monster facing north also sees a target directly to its side. A few creatures see in all directions and skip this test entirely.
  3. You are close enough. Each type has a sight range. Darkness halves it, in proportion to how dark the screen actually is — the same value that dims your viewport shortens their vision. Some creatures see in the dark and are exempt. Even in pitch black, a monster still sees one cell.
  4. There is a clear line. Walls block. Closed doors block, unless the door is one of the two "special" types the level designer marked as passable.

For a group, only distinct facings are tested. Four monsters all looking north perform one test, not four.

The line-of-sight test is worth describing because it is not a ray cast. It walks from you towards the monster, one cell at a time, choosing at each step whether to move along x or y by whichever keeps the slope of the line closest to the original. If it walks into a wall the path is blocked — except on an exact tie between the two choices, where it gets one retry along the other diagonal. Perfect diagonals are special-cased: both orthogonal neighbours and the corner cell must be blocked before the line is refused, so monsters can see you round the inside of a corner.

Smell

If sight fails, a monster can still find you two ways.

Close range. Within roughly half its smelling range, and with a clear line that ignores doors, it simply knows where you are. This is the mechanic that makes hiding behind a closed door unreliable: the door stops them seeing you, not smelling you.

Your trail. The game remembers the last 24 squares the party stood on, each with a scent strength. The square you step onto starts at 24; leaving a square adds the time you spent there, up to 80; and every ten seconds every scent but the one under you fades by one, the oldest going when it reaches nothing. A monster standing on one of those squares may follow it. The test is a random roll plus the scent's strength against a threshold — and the threshold falls as the creature's nose improves, so a keen-nosed monster will follow fainter, older trails. A worm has by far the best nose in the game and can scarcely see at all.

That is the design. No release of the game ever kept the strengths that way: see the footprint trail under Solved.

Four moods

Behaviour is dominated by a single small state I will call the group's mood. There are four in use.

MoodWhat it meansWhat it does
CalmNothing is happeningWanders; hunts by smell if it catches your trail
FleeingPanickingRuns away, faster than it hunts
EngagedIn your faceFormation breaks — each member acts alone
ChargingComing for youHeads for where it last knew you to be

The code knows more. It tests in several places for two other moods — call them Wary, whose monster only pauses when it sees you, and Alert, whose monster charges — and resets a group to Calm from anything "at or above" a value no mood reaches. Nothing in the game ever puts a group in either, so those branches never run.

The transitions are the interesting part:

  • You attack it, or walk into it → Engaged, unless it is already Engaged or Fleeing. This is the fastest way to get a monster's full attention.
  • It takes damage → if already Fleeing or Engaged, nothing changes. Otherwise it checks whether it can see you; if it cannot, it just turns on the spot, and if it can, it tries to move.
  • Calm, and it sees you at range → Charging.
  • It reaches the square where it last saw you and you are not there → Calm. The trail has gone cold.
  • A group member dies while Engaged → the survivors may drop to Fleeing, and are likelier to the more of them have already died. Bravery is a per-type number; at the maximum a creature never breaks, whatever happens.
  • Fleeing runs out — the panic is a countdown — or a fleeing monster is cornered next to you with nowhere to go → Calm. It stops panicking and turns to face you.
Why this one detail matters

Engaged is the only mood in which individual members get their own turns. Several other parts of the code only bother renumbering or clearing per-member state when the group is Engaged — because that is the only time per-member state exists at all.

One turn, as a decision tree

A group's turn arrives as a scheduled event. Roughly:

Is this group's level the party's level?
├─ No ──▶ only the leader acts: one random step, then sleep
│         (longer the further the party's level is)
└─ Yes
   │
   ├─ Is any member at zero health?
   │  └─ Yes ──▶ remove it, re-queue this turn for right now, stop
   │             (the group reconsiders once it has shrunk)
   │
   ├─ Is life magically frozen, and this creature not invincible?
   │  └─ Yes ──▶ do nothing, try again in a moment
   │
   └─ Act, according to why this turn happened:
      │
      ├─ "I was attacked"  ──▶ break formation, everyone turn to face you
      │
      ├─ "I was damaged"   ──▶ can I see them?
      │                        ├─ no  ──▶ turn on the spot, reschedule
      │                        └─ yes ──▶ try to move
      │
      ├─ "A cloud or door hurt me"
      │                     ──▶ try every direction from a random start
      │
      ├─ An individual member's turn (only when Engaged)
      │                     ──▶ see "Deciding to attack"
      │
      └─ The group's ordinary turn ──▶ dispatch on mood

The ordinary group turn, expanded:

Mood?
├─ Fleeing  ──▶ run away
│
├─ Charging ──▶ can I see them?
│               ├─ yes, close enough, lined up ──▶ break formation
│               ├─ yes, otherwise ──▶ close in at double speed
│               └─ no  ──▶ head for where I last saw them
│
├─ Calm     ──▶ can I see them?
│               ├─ yes, close enough and lined up ──▶ break formation
│               ├─ yes, otherwise ──▶ become Charging, act next tick
│               └─ no  ──▶ can I smell them, or their trail?
│                          ├─ yes ──▶ move that way
│                          └─ no  ──▶ coin flip:
│                                      ├─ wander in a random direction
│                                      └─ try all four from a random start
│
└─ Engaged ──▶ (the group does little; members act on their own turns)

"Break formation" means: mood becomes Engaged, note where the party is, and give every member its own turn — member 0 always turns to face you, the rest on a coin flip.

Choosing a direction

When a monster has somewhere it wants to go, it tries four directions in a fixed order, taking the first that works:

  1. The direction it wants.
  2. The secondary direction — when the target is off at an angle, the other of the two directions bracketing it, chosen at random when the angle is an exact diagonal.
  3. That secondary reversed.
  4. The wanted direction reversed — but only one time in four.

If none work, it turns to face where it wanted to go and waits out a move interval. Monsters therefore look like they are "thinking" when boxed in: they face the right way and shuffle.

Two touches make movement feel less mechanical. It will not immediately double back to the square it just left — or rather, it will one time in four, which is what stops monsters oscillating between two cells. And a step only happens if enough time has passed; if not, the monster keeps the turn but shortens its own next interval, so its motion stays smooth rather than stuttering.

A step is refused by walls, stairs, open pits (unless the creature floats), closed false walls, doors too far shut for a creature of that height, another monster, and by the party. Some creatures recognise illusory pits and false walls and will walk onto them; most are fooled. Every creature walks into an open teleporter, but a few wary types — the vexirk, zytaz, demon, dragon and the Lords — refuse one that would carry them to a level their kind is not allowed on, which would kill them.

Speed is emotional

Fleeing is a quarter faster than normal, and charging is twice as fast. A cornered monster that turns to fight is genuinely slower than the same monster running away from you.

Deciding to attack

An individual member, on its own turn, and not already mid-swing:

Can it see the party?
│
├─ no ──▶ can the group see them?
│         ├─ yes ──▶ turn towards them, wait
│         └─ no  ──▶ become Charging, head for last known position
│
└─ yes
   │
   ├─ Is it facing them? (or does it see in all directions?)
   │   └─ no ──▶ as above
   │
   ├─ Is the party within this creature's reach?
   ├─ Are they orthogonally lined up? (same row or column)
   ├─ Does it win a roll against its own reach?
   │   └─ any "no" ──▶ become Charging, close in at double speed
   │
   └─ all yes ──▶ ATTACK

Before a melee attack — reach 1 only — a monster standing in an awkward corner of its cell will often sidestep to a better one first, and that shuffle can walk it onto a missile you left lying there. Alone in its group, the Atari's monster half the time takes the centre of the cell instead; CSBWin's never does, and the port follows CSBWin there for now. The vexirk, which may strike any champion, skips the shuffle three times in four and strikes from the back corner.

The attack is a form, not a decision

The attack itself is a filled-in block of parameters: who to hit, whether to throw something instead, what to throw, how far, how hard, which sound. The block is handed to a dungeon script that may rewrite any field, and only then acted on. That indirection is why custom dungeons can make monsters behave in ways the original never did.

Who gets hit is either the nearest reachable champion — determined by which side of the cell the blow comes from, then a fixed order per side — or, for the creature types with a "strike anyone" flag, a randomly chosen living champion regardless of position.

Whether it lands has two independent escapes before damage is even rolled: being quicker than the attacker (and still needing to win a 3-in-4 roll), and being lucky. Luck is a spendable resource — succeeding at a luck check lowers your luck.

How much it hurts goes through a deliberately convoluted chain: a base from the creature's attack rating, reduced by twice your fighting skill, then halved, then grown by three separate random terms, then quartered, then possibly reduced again. Armour then scales it, averaged over the body parts the blow could land on. A large enough hit also wounds a body part, and because the wound threshold doubles each time, one big blow can mark several places while a hundred small ones mark none.

Four genuine behavioural differences, rather than numeric ones:

The giggler does not damage you. It steals an item and, having stolen, is put into a special agitated state and runs. It is also the only creature in the game with zero bravery, so it flees at the slightest provocation.

The throwers — slime devil, flying eye, vexirk, zytaz, demon, dragon, Lord Chaos — each have a fixed repertoire. A vexirk or Lord Chaos rolls between a fireball and one of four spells; a flying eye throws lightning seven times in eight and something else otherwise. A thrower standing next to you will sometimes punch instead, on a coin flip. Everything else in the game has no ranged attack at all.

The Deth Knight is the only creature that visibly flips its sprite mid-attack, on a coin toss, with a sound cue.

The three aspects of the Lord

Lord Chaos, Lord Order and the Grey Lord are one being in three aspects, which is why all three share a single identical set of statistics. The endgame is a transformation between them, and it explains a great deal about how they are written.

Lord Chaos alone is a creature you can meet and fight. Fusing him with Lord Order — the being CSBWin leaves unnamed, and the aspect representing pure order — produces the Grey Lord, and the Grey Lord is not an opponent at all. It appears, the game is won, and it ends.

The fusion sequence bears that out step for step: the game-won flag is set at the very start, all three party shields are raised to 100, fireball clouds swell, and the creature ahead of you flickers between Chaos and Order twelve times, slowing as it goes, before settling as the Grey Lord. Every other monster on the level is then deleted, the dungeon's text is read out, and the game prints "THE END." and rolls the credits.

Two apparent defects, explained

The Grey Lord has a long reach, which would normally qualify it to throw something, yet it appears in no table of what any creature throws — because it is never meant to act. And the check that stops an invincible creature taking turns once the game is won exists precisely so that, in the seconds between the Grey Lord appearing and the credits, it stands there and does nothing.

Lord Chaos himself is a different animal to everything else you actually fight:

  • He cannot be damaged at all. Nothing you do reduces his health.
  • He moves two squares at a time, and buzzes as he arrives.
  • The only thing that blocks him is a Fluxcage. That is not a general mechanic — the fluxcage check sits inside an "am I invincible?" branch, so a fluxcage is inert against every other creature in the game. It exists for this one purpose: herding him into position for the fusion.
  • If he ends up somewhere he cannot be placed, he gets a 25% chance per retry to wander one square and try again — so he can escape a corner you have walled him into, given time.

Two-corner and cell-filling creatures (worms, hellhounds; dragons, scorpions, couatls, golems, elementals, the Lords) constrain group size — you cannot have four dragons in a cell — and two-corner creatures must turn both halves together.

Unsolved

Things nobody has explained yet. Christophe, of the Dungeon Master Encyclopaedia and the author of ReDMCSB, checked this section against the Atari's own code and explained most of what it held; that is under Solved, below. Paul Stevens' comments show he hit several of the same walls, and I have quoted him where he did.

A flag on creatures it cannot affect

One per-type flag lets a quarter-cell creature strike from the back of its cell, and only together with the "strike anyone" flag; the vexirk alone has both. The screamer has it without the other, where it does nothing, and so do the water elemental, the dragon and all three aspects of the Lord, which fill their cell, where the code never looks at it. Christophe's guess is that the flag once meant something else and the data was never cleared.

Why wariness is a number

Whether a creature refuses a teleporter that would kill it is decided by a per-type number, which ReDMCSB calls wariness, being at least 10: the vexirk, zytaz, demon, dragon and the Lords pass. Nothing else reads the field, so a simple flag would have done. FTL perhaps meant it for more, and never used it.

Solved

The condition that cannot fail

One branch tests whether the mood is Calm or Alert and not Charging — but the first test already excludes Charging. Stevens wrote:

"The following line makes no sense. But I have studied the original machine language over and over again. This is what it did."

It is redundant, not unreachable, and harmless: Alert never occurs, so the real test is just "Calm". ReDMCSB marks it as useless code, most likely a leftover from the many rounds of tuning the creatures' behaviour.

The footprint trail

The routine that writes the scent strengths gets Stevens' bluntest comment:

"I think this is much hogwash. Does nothing useful. Is full of errors. Perhaps an old, abandoned debug trace."

It is broken in four ways, all in the original:

  • On the Atari, the square is never compared. The compiler FTL used, Megamax C, accepted comparisons of whole structures and threw their result away. So every step sets every strength along the trail to 24, the time spent anywhere is lost, and the ten-second fade spares only the newest scent, not every scent of the square you stand on.
  • The strengths are written backwards. The loop walks the squares up from the oldest while it walks the strengths down from the newest, so it changes the strength of square n at k − 1 − n. On the Atari the first bug hides it; the Amiga, whose compiler compares, shows it.
  • A pit or a teleporter to another level records the arrival twice: once by the fall, once as the new level is loaded. Two of the 24 places for one square. Stairs record it once.
  • The footprint spell's two ends are swapped. The code means a weak YA BRO ROS to show the squares walked from the cast, and a strong one to show the whole trail. Every power shows only what is walked from the cast.

CSBWin adds two of its own: it never writes the oldest scent's strength, and its fade stops for the tick after dropping a scent. And the routine that drops a scent, which I first called wrong as well, is fine: CSBWin's copy routine takes its source first, and I had read it the other way round.

The port plays what the code means: the time spent counts, an arrival is recorded once, and a strong spell shows the whole trail. It plays CSBWin's loops only for CSBWin's own recordings, which need them to play on.

A branch that seemed to do nothing but consume randomness

In the pre-melee sidestep there is a 50% branch whose only statement is commented out in CSBWin. In the original it is not empty: the commented line, written with the old name of the byte that holds each member's corner, moves a lone monster to the centre of its cell, and the Atari's does just that. Stevens left no note on why he took it out; it is already out in CSBWin 11.014, an older source than the one the port follows. The port follows CSBWin there for now, and will do the Atari's for its own games.

Four moods are never used

Of eight possible values, one never appears in the code at all. Three more appear — Wary, Alert, and the value of the "reset anything at or above this" cleanup — but nothing ever sets them. Only four moods occur in play.

Undocumented flags that turned out to matter

Two per-type flags are marked only "????" in CSBWin. One lets a creature strike any champion: it picks a living one at random, where any other strikes the champion the side of its cell faces. The other lets a quarter-cell creature strike from the back of its cell, but only with the first: the vexirk, the one creature with both, steps forward one time in four and strikes from where it stands the other three. Why the other types carry it is still unsolved.

A value read before it is written

Stevens patched the Atari binary to give a variable a fixed value, noting that the uninitialised value was usually "part of an address" and that leaving it made the two builds impossible to compare. When a cloud or a closing door hurts a group, the value is read in a test that the same event makes false anyway. When a missile hits one, the event falls through into the same code, and if the group cannot move at once, the leftover value decides whether it turns. Being part of an address, it is large, and the group does not turn — exactly what Stevens' fixed value gives, which the port uses. A real bug, but in practice probably without effect.

A number reused as two different things

In the corner-shuffling code one value is used as both a position and a bitfield within a few lines. Stevens: "I think there is something wrong with D5 here." There is not. It saves a register: the random bits tested and the corner stored never overlap, and storing the corner clears the bits tested next. A lone monster off the centre goes to the centre seven times in eight, or to the next or previous corner; one already in the centre goes to a random corner one time in eight.

What wariness decides

I had it as whether a creature will step into a teleporter at all. Every creature does. A creature whose wariness is at least 10 only refuses an open teleporter that carries creatures to a level its kind is not allowed on, where arriving would kill it. Why that field is a number is still unsolved.

What this adds up to

The impression from outside is that Dungeon Master's monsters are cunning. Having read the code, I would say the effect comes from four cheap tricks layered well:

  1. Senses that fail differently. Sight is blocked by doors and darkness, smell is not. So a monster that has lost you often still knows roughly where you went, and the two failure modes together read as searching.
  2. Commitment. A monster that has seen you keeps heading for where you were, and only calms down on arriving and finding nothing. That single behaviour produces most of the game's best moments.
  3. Reluctance to backtrack. One rule — do not immediately re-enter the square you just left — removes nearly all of the twitching that would otherwise expose the machinery.
  4. Speed tied to intent. Fleeing is faster than hunting; charging is faster still. The pace of a fight changes as the monster's mood changes, and that reads as emotion.

None of it is planning. There is no pathfinding — a monster that cannot see a way towards you simply tries four directions and gives up. Put a pillar between you and a rock pile and it will lose you completely. But the failure modes are quiet, and the successes are legible, which is the whole trick.
